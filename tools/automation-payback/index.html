Method, and the assumptions that produced these numbers

The gate comes first

An option whose total installed cost exceeds the gate is removed before anything is scored, not penalised inside the score. A project you cannot fund is not a weak project; it is not a candidate. This is why the best-looking machine on the list can disappear entirely when you tighten the gate by a few thousand dollars.

Anchors are absolute

Each component is scored against a fixed threshold, never against the rest of the field: payback 0.75 yr → 100, 4.0 yr → 0; lifetime NPV per dollar invested 4.0× → 100; IRR 75% → 100 with your discount rate as the floor; capital preserved as the share of the gate left unspent. Adding a weak option to the list therefore cannot raise anyone else's score — a shortlist cannot be padded to make a favourite look better.

Weights

  • Payback — 30
  • NPV per dollar invested — 35
  • IRR — 20
  • Capital preserved — 15

Capital preserved is weighted deliberately low: unspent budget has real option value, but not enough to overturn an option that is better on return. The ranking is checked against strict dominance — if one option costs no more and is at least as good on payback, NPV and IRR, it cannot be ranked below.

The 125-case sweep

Three drivers, five levels each: benefit realisation (60–100% of the savings you claimed), labor rate (±20%), and capital overrun (0–40% above quoted). Every one of the 125 combinations is re-gated and re-ranked from scratch, so an overrun can push an option out of the budget entirely. The figure reported is how often your base-case winner is still the winner.

What this does not model

  • Taxes and depreciation. Cash flows are pre-tax. A depreciation shield raises NPV for the capital-heavy options, so a close race can flip.
  • Ramp. Savings are treated as starting at full rate in year 1. A cell needing a fixture and a taught program per part number will not.
  • Constraint location. Hours saved at a non-bottleneck operation do not become throughput. This tool cannot tell which of your operations is the constraint; a capacity model can.
  • Risk of non-delivery. Integration schedule, operator acceptance and vendor support are not scored.

Definitions

  • Invested = capital + installation + training.
  • Net/yr = (hours saved/wk × 52 × rate) + scrap avoided + (added units × margin) − recurring cost.
  • Payback = invested ÷ net annual, undiscounted.
  • NPV discounts the net annual over the useful life at your hurdle rate.
  • IRR solved by bisection on the same annuity; blank when the nominal outlay is never recovered.

This screens a shortlist. It does not tell you where your constraint is.

The hard part was never the payback arithmetic — it is knowing which operation is actually short, whether the hours you are saving are on the bottleneck, and what your true capacity is once you stop letting the plan load machines past 100%.
